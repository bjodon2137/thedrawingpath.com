Phase 1 · Foundation

Panel Composition

Goals for This Lesson
  • Understand the function of a comics panel and the gutter between them
  • Compose six different shot types: wide, medium, close-up, extreme close-up, establishing, insert
  • Control reader attention through framing — what to show, what to leave out
  • Draw two single pages that demonstrate your panel compositions
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Shot Types
  • Draw each of the 6 shot types 3 times
  • Study a published comic: identify shot types used per page
  • Thumbnail a 4-panel scene with deliberate shot variety
  • Note how shot changes affect reader experience
Week 2: Composition Practice
  • Fill a sketchbook page with 12 mini-panels, each a different scene
  • Redraw one panel from a comic you love with a different shot type
  • Compose a 6-panel page with no dialogue — tell a story visually
  • Experiment: same scene, 3 different compositions. Which works best?
Key Concepts

The Panel as a Window

Each panel is a frozen moment the artist chooses to show

A panel shows one moment in time. It has four edges — each edge is a decision about what to include or exclude.

The panel edge is an active compositional tool. Push important elements toward edges for tension; center them for stability.

Consider negative space within the panel, sight lines, and how the panel's visual weight balances across the page spread.

Six Essential Shot Types

Coverage borrowed from film — each serves a storytelling purpose

Establishing (wide, sets location), medium (character + some context), close-up (face/reaction), and insert (important object).

Extreme wide shots create scale and loneliness. Extreme close-ups create intimacy or unease. Use contrast between shot sizes for impact.

Shot selection tells the reader what matters. A scene about dialogue uses more mediums and close-ups; action sequences vary size dynamically.

The Gutter

The space between panels where the reader's imagination does the work

Readers fill in the action between panels. This mental leap is called 'closure' — it's what makes comics uniquely interactive.

Gutters can vary in size: wide gutters slow pacing, narrow gutters speed it up. Borderless panels can bleed into each other.

Scott McCloud's 'Understanding Comics' identifies 6 types of panel-to-panel transitions. Mastering these gives you precise narrative control.

Framing as Meaning

How you frame a subject changes what readers feel about it

Low angle (looking up) = power/dominance. High angle (looking down) = vulnerability. Straight on = neutrality.

Dutch angle (tilted horizon) signals unease or chaos. Wide framing suggests loneliness; tight framing suggests claustrophobia or intensity.

Every framing choice is an editorial comment. Ask: what do I want the reader to feel in this panel? Then choose the angle that achieves it.

Phase 1 · Foundation

Visual Storytelling Basics

Goals for This Lesson
  • Understand panel-to-panel transitions: action-to-action, subject-to-subject, scene-to-scene
  • Tell a complete story using only 4–6 panels and no words
  • Use eyelines, staging, and visual cause-and-effect to lead readers through a sequence
  • Identify and avoid the most common beginner storytelling mistakes
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Silent Sequences
  • Read and analyze 3 wordless comic sequences (Shaun Tan, Will Eisner)
  • Draw a 4-panel chase with only stick figures — focus on clear action
  • Draw a 4-panel emotional exchange: two characters, no words
  • Note which panels need establishing shots and which don't
Week 2: Clarity & Flow
  • Re-draw your week 1 sequences with more polished figures
  • Swap panels out of order — does the sequence still read? Why?
  • Add one twist or surprise to your silent story
  • Get 3 readers to describe what happened in your sequence
Key Concepts

Panel-to-Panel Transitions

The 6 types of transition McCloud identified, each with different storytelling power

Action-to-action (punch → land) is clearest. Subject-to-subject (different characters) requires the gutter to connect. Scene-to-scene leaps in time or space.

Aspect-to-aspect panels (common in manga) show different views of the same moment, building atmosphere. Non-sequitur transitions break logic — used for surreal or dream sequences.

Deliberate use of transition types is professional-level storytelling. Most beginners default to action-to-action. Mixing types creates pace and variety.

Eyeline and Staging

Characters' sight lines guide the reader across the gutter

If character A looks right in one panel, character B should appear on the right side of the next panel. Broken eyelines confuse readers.

The 180° rule (from film): keep characters on consistent sides of an imaginary line so readers always know who's talking to whom.

Staging is where you place characters in the panel space. Front, middle, background relationships create depth and power dynamics simultaneously.

Show Don't Tell

In visual storytelling, images should do the narrative heavy lifting

If a character is sad, show slumped posture, averted eyes, rain on a window — rather than a caption that says 'She was sad.'

Environment and props can externalize internal states: a messy room for a chaotic mind, a spotlight for isolation.

Beginners over-rely on dialogue to explain what the art should show. Practice: take a scene you'd write in text and convert it entirely to images.

Visual Economy

Every panel should earn its place — remove anything that doesn't advance story or character

Ask of each panel: does this advance plot, reveal character, or build atmosphere? If none of those, cut it.

Redundancy: don't draw what a caption already says. Trust the image. Overlapping narration wastes panel real estate.

One idea per panel is a good rule when starting out. As you develop fluency, you can layer multiple story beats into a single image.

Phase 1 · Foundation

Comic Page Layout

Goals for This Lesson
  • Design a full page from panel grid to splash layout — understanding how the page itself tells the story
  • Understand the difference between a grid, a modular layout, and a splash or double-page spread
  • Use panel size to control pacing and emphasis across a full page
  • Thumbnail a complete 6-page short comic from scratch
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Thumbnails
  • Thumbnail 10 different page layouts — vary grid, size, shape of panels
  • Study 5 pages from comics you admire: map their panel grid
  • Draw the same 6-panel story content on three different page layouts
  • Note how layout change affects the reading experience
Week 2: Full Pages
  • Thumbnail a 6-page short comic beginning to end
  • Choose your best layout approach and draw 2 polished pages
  • Design a splash page (single dominant image)
  • Design a double-page spread — how do the two pages read together?
Key Concepts

The Page as a Unit

Readers see the full page before reading it — layout is the first visual impression

A regular 6-panel grid reads as orderly and controlled. Breaking that grid signals something important or chaotic.

The top of the page is an establishing zone; the bottom-right panel is the 'exit' — it should push the reader to turn the page.

Page design is architectural. Balance the visual weight: don't put all your large panels on one side. The eye should travel naturally across the page.

Splash Pages and Spreads

When one image demands the whole page — or two pages

A splash page is a full-page panel used for impact: chapter openers, major reveals, climactic moments.

Double-page spreads combine two facing pages into one image. They require planning, since the gutter (binding) splits the center.

Avoid splash pages for unimportant moments — save them for your most powerful beats. Overuse drains their power.

Tiers and Horizontal Bands

Dividing the page into horizontal tiers controls reading rhythm

A 3-tier page (3 rows) is the workhorse of superhero comics. Each tier functions like a sentence in a paragraph.

Varying tier heights within a page adds visual interest: short tiers read fast, tall tiers slow the eye.

Panels can cross tier boundaries — a tall vertical panel might span 2 tiers, creating a visual anchor that organizes surrounding smaller panels.

Thumbnailing Pages

Small rough sketches that plan the full comic before detailed drawing

Thumbnails are 2–3 inches tall and take 5 minutes each. They let you test layouts before committing to penciling.

Thumbnail the whole story before drawing a single finished panel. This prevents layout problems discovered on page 8 that require redrawing page 3.

Professional approach: thumbnail → rough pencils → tight pencils → inks. The thumbnail is the director's blueprint — everything that follows executes that plan.

Phase 1 · Foundation

Lettering & Dialogue

Goals for This Lesson
  • Letter by hand and understand the rules for speech balloons, thought balloons, and captions
  • Write dialogue that sounds natural and doesn't overlap with what the art shows
  • Size and place lettering so it doesn't fight the artwork
  • Understand how sound effects (SFX) function as visual elements
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Lettering Practice
  • Learn the hand-lettering basics: uppercase, consistent letterforms, even spacing
  • Letter 10 different speech balloons with varying text lengths
  • Practice caption boxes: narration, internal monologue, location labels
  • Draw 3 thought balloons — experiment with cloud vs. broken-line styles
Week 2: Integration
  • Letter a full 4-panel page — text first, then adjust art to fit
  • Write and letter a dialogue-heavy scene: two characters talking for 2 pages
  • Create 5 SFX that visually match their sounds (CRACK, WHOOSH, etc.)
  • Try digital lettering with Clip Studio or Affinity if available
Key Concepts

Balloon Types and Their Meanings

Different balloon shapes carry different narrative meanings

Round/oval balloon: normal speech. Jagged spikes: shouting or alarm. Wavy/wobbly outline: weak, whispered, or electronic. Cloud with small circles: thought.

The tail of a speech balloon points to the speaker. Multiple tails from one balloon indicate multiple speakers saying the same thing.

Captions (rectangular boxes) are for narration — they have no tail. Use them for setting description, past-tense narration, or a character's internal thoughts in script style.

Hand Lettering Rules

Consistency makes lettering read like a designed element, not an afterthought

Use all caps for standard comics lettering. Keep letter height consistent (use pencil guide lines). Space letters optically, not mathematically.

Emphasis: bold a word by making it heavier. Don't use italics in hand lettering — instead, use a line under the word or bold it.

Never letter so small it's hard to read at printed size. A good test: arm's length, can you read every word? If not, it's too small.

Writing for the Balloon

Comics dialogue has less space than a novel — every word must work harder

Read your dialogue out loud. If it sounds like a book, rewrite it until it sounds like speech.

Avoid explaining what the art already shows ('I can see that building is on fire!'). Comics dialogue often communicates what the art can't: subtext, backstory, emotion under the surface.

The best comics dialogue adds a layer on top of the image — two things happening simultaneously. Art shows the physical reality; dialogue reveals the interior or the subtext.

Sound Effects as Design

SFX are both language and visual art — they should feel appropriate to the scene

Match the visual style of your SFX to your overall art style. A realistic SFX looks different from a cartoonish one.

Integrate SFX into the panel — they should feel like they're happening in the scene, not pasted on top.

Professional letterers treat SFX as typography. Study Todd Klein, Nate Piekos (Blambot), and Richard Starkings (Comicraft) for how to do this at a high level.

Phase 2 · Intermediate

Sequential Gesture & Continuity

Goals for This Lesson
  • Draw the same character moving through 6 poses across 6 panels with consistent anatomy
  • Understand action lines, impact frames, and how to convey motion in a still image
  • Master the 'in-between' problem: the reader must believe the gap between panels
  • Draw a 2-page action sequence with clear, readable motion
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Motion Studies
  • Draw the same figure throwing a punch across 4 panels — every key frame
  • Study action panels from Neal Adams, Jack Kirby, or John Romita Jr.
  • Draw a character running: panel 1 (start), panel 4 (mid-stride), panel 6 (stop)
  • Practice impact frames: the moment of contact, with motion blur and impact lines
Week 2: Continuity Sequences
  • Draw a 6-panel action scene: character jumps across a gap
  • Focus on consistent character proportions across all 6 panels
  • Add speed lines, impact marks, and motion trails appropriately
  • Challenge: draw the same sequence from 3 different camera angles
Key Concepts

Action Lines and Impact Marks

Visual conventions that sell motion in a static image

Speed lines radiate from the trailing edge of a moving object. Impact marks (star bursts) show collision points. Both are shorthand readers understand instantly.

Vary the density of speed lines to indicate speed: sparse lines = slow; dense, overlapping lines = very fast.

Don't overuse impact marks — they're punctuation. Save the starburst for real moments of impact; use simple motion blur for continuous movement.

Key Frames Across Panels

Choosing which moments to freeze in time

Think of each panel as a key frame in an animation. Choose the pose that communicates the action most clearly — usually the extreme position, not the in-between.

For a punch: panel 1 shows the wind-up (or the moment before), not a mid-swing blur. Panel 2 shows the impact. The reader fills in the swing.

The 'Kirby Krackle' principle: exaggerate the key pose. Comics action is more extreme than real movement — it has to read at a glance.

Tools for Character Consistency

How professionals keep the same character looking the same across 100 pages

Keep a character model sheet nearby while drawing. Check proportions against it every 2–3 panels.

Identify 3–4 key landmarks unique to your character (scar, earring, specific hairstyle) and make sure they appear consistently.

When in doubt, trace your own previous panel. Professionals do this routinely — it's not cheating, it's consistency control.

Staging Action Sequences

How to make fight scenes, chases, and action read clearly

Establish geography first: a wide shot showing the space before the action begins. Readers need to know where everything is before things start moving.

Maintain directional consistency: if character A is moving left, they should continue moving left throughout the sequence unless something reverses their direction.

Vary your shots during action: wide shot → close up → medium → detail → wide. Monotonous shot selection makes action feel static even when the poses are dynamic.

Phase 2 · Intermediate

Character Consistency Across Pages

Goals for This Lesson
  • Build a complete character model sheet: front, 3/4, profile, back views plus key expressions
  • Develop a set of 3 distinct characters who look clearly different at any scale
  • Draw the same character in 3 different lighting conditions without losing recognizability
  • Understand how costume and silhouette carry character identity across a long comic
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Model Sheets
  • Draw your protagonist from 4 angles: front, 3/4, side, back
  • Draw a 6-expression sheet: neutral, happy, angry, fearful, surprised, sad
  • Note the 5 key visual landmarks that define your character
  • Draw your character at 3 different sizes (full body, waist-up, head)
Week 2: Cast Development
  • Design 2 more characters who contrast with your protagonist in silhouette
  • Draw all 3 characters standing together — do they read as distinct?
  • Draw a scene with all 3 interacting in the same panel
  • Test: can someone identify each character from a thumbnail-sized version?
Key Concepts

The Silhouette Test

If you can't identify the character in solid black, the design isn't strong enough

Fill your character with solid black and hold it at arm's length. Can you identify them? Costume shape, hair, and body type must be distinct.

Classic examples: Batman's cowl and cape, Spider-Man's webbing, Wolverine's claws and hair — all read as silhouettes.

When designing a cast, ensure every character's silhouette is unique. A lineup of similar-shaped characters is the most common beginner design failure.

What Goes on a Model Sheet

The reference document that keeps your character consistent across a long work

A standard model sheet includes: front, 3/4, side, and back views at the same height; a size comparison with other characters; and an expression chart.

Add notes to the model sheet: hair color, eye color, costume colors, any tattoos or markings, height relative to other characters.

Professional model sheets include 'do' and 'don't' examples — common mistakes to avoid. This is helpful if you ever work with an inker or colorist.

Costume as Character

What characters wear communicates who they are without a word

Costume should reflect personality: a neat, buttoned-up shirt for a controlled character; a disheveled jacket for a chaotic one.

Practical detail: make sure your character's costume is drawable from memory after a few practice runs. Overly complex designs slow page production.

Silhouette-defining elements (capes, hats, distinctive bags) help readers find a character in a crowded panel. Functional accessories (tools, weapons) tell us about their role.

Facial Landmarks for Consistency

The 3–4 features that make a face recognizable across a comic

Pick distinct features and stick to them: the shape of the nose, the set of the eyes, a unique jaw line, the eyebrows. These are the fixed points readers track.

Expressions change many things — but not the underlying bone structure. Practice drawing your character's face angry, happy, and sad, and check that the underlying structure is still recognizable.

Avoid giving all your characters the same default face with different hair and clothes. Faces in real life are extremely varied — push that variety in your character designs.

Phase 2 · Intermediate

Inking for Comics

Goals for This Lesson
  • Understand the function of ink in comics: line quality, value, and clarity
  • Practice 3 core inking techniques: brush line, crosshatching, and spotting blacks
  • Ink a full penciled page, making deliberate decisions about every mark
  • Explore digital inking as a parallel workflow
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Technique Drills
  • Fill a sketchbook page with ink line exercises: thick-to-thin, pressure variation
  • Practice crosshatching: parallel, cross, contour, and stipple
  • Spot blacks on a simple scene sketch — push to 70% coverage in shadow areas
  • Ink 5 identical penciled figures with different inking styles
Week 2: Full Page Inking
  • Pencil and then ink a complete 4-panel page
  • Try feathering and dry brush for texture on a background element
  • Ink the same page digitally (Procreate, Clip Studio) and compare
  • Study an inker you admire: identify 3 specific techniques they use
Key Concepts

Line Weight Hierarchy

Thicker outer lines, thinner interior lines — the grammar of comics ink

The general rule: outline a figure with your heaviest line, then interior details with medium lines, and the smallest details with your finest lines.

Line weight variation makes flat ink drawings feel three-dimensional. A single-weight line reads as flat and amateur; varied weight reads as professional.

Shadow-side edges of forms traditionally get slightly heavier line weight, reinforcing the lighting. This is subtle but adds depth without any shading.

Spotting Blacks

Strategic placement of solid black areas to control value and focus

Black areas advance visually; white areas recede. Spot your blacks to direct the reader's eye toward the most important story element in the panel.

The '60/30/10 rule' adapted for ink: 60% midtone (crosshatch), 30% paper-white, 10% solid black — but adjust for mood. Noir comics might be 60% black.

Common beginner mistake: too little black. When in doubt, go darker. You can always lighten with white-out; adding black to a printed page is impossible.

Brush vs. Pen — Choosing Your Tool

Different tools produce different line characters

A sable brush (size 2 or 3) gives the most expressive line: thick at the belly, thin at the tip. It's the classic comics inking tool but requires practice.

Dip pens and tech pens give more consistent, controllable lines — better for beginners and for architecture, backgrounds, and lettering.

Digital brushes in Clip Studio Paint simulate both options and are non-destructive. Many working professionals ink entirely digitally now.

Texture and Atmosphere Through Mark

Crosshatch, feathering, dry brush, and stipple for varied surface textures

Crosshatch creates shadow gradients. Feathering (lines that taper off) creates soft shadow edges. Dry brush (nearly empty brush dragged fast) creates rough textures.

Each technique takes practice to do well. Devote a full sketchbook page to each, independently, before using them in comics.

Match your texture technique to your scene: crosshatch for controlled interiors, dry brush for aged surfaces and dramatic weather, stipple for delicate shading.

Phase 2 · Intermediate

Color & Mood in Sequential Art

Goals for This Lesson
  • Apply color to a finished inked page using a limited 3-color palette
  • Understand how hue, temperature, and value control emotional tone in a scene
  • Use color to guide the reader's eye and separate foreground from background
  • Design a color script for a 6-page story: different emotional palette for each scene
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Color Foundations
  • Flat-color a 4-panel page: assign one base color per panel
  • Recolor the same page for 3 different emotional moods
  • Study how Dave Stewart, Laura Martin, or Jordie Bellaire use limited palettes
  • Practice adding a single ambient light source to your flat colors
Week 2: Color Script
  • Write a 6-scene color script: map emotion to palette for each scene
  • Draw and color 2 pages with a deliberate temperature shift (warm to cool)
  • Add cast shadow and rim light to a colored character
  • Experiment with panel-specific color vs. consistent page palette
Key Concepts

Color Temperature and Emotion

Warm and cool colors carry emotional associations readers feel instinctively

Warm (red, orange, yellow): danger, energy, passion, conflict. Cool (blue, green, purple): calm, sadness, mystery, isolation.

The same scene colored warm vs. cool will read as completely different stories. Try it on your own panels.

Mixed temperature lighting (warm key light + cool fill light, or vice versa) creates visual depth and is a cinematic technique used by colorists like Jordie Bellaire.

The Power of Limited Palettes

Restricting your color choices creates visual harmony and thematic coherence

A 3-color palette (one dominant, one supporting, one accent) is more visually satisfying than 10 colors pulled at random.

Monochromatic stories (like Sin City's selective color, or Inio Asano's work) use color strategically as emphasis, making it more powerful when it appears.

Professional colorists often plan color keys before touching a page: which colors belong to which characters, locations, or emotional states.

Value — The Most Important Element

Even before hue, value contrast is what separates foreground from background

In greyscale, does your colored image still read clearly? If your character disappears into the background, you have a value problem.

Rule of thumb: keep your foreground element a different value from its immediate background. Light figure on dark ground, or dark figure on light ground.

Color is secondary to value. Professional tip: desaturate your colored page and check the value read before calling it finished.

Color Scripts

Planning the full emotional arc of a story through color before rendering a panel

A color script (borrowed from animation) is a row of small thumbnails showing the palette of each scene across the story.

It prevents accidental color monotony and ensures your story has visual contrast and development — the palette should evolve as the story does.

Pixar pioneered the color script as a storytelling tool. Comics colorists like Matt Wilson (Saga) use similar planning for long-form series.

World-Building Through Visual Design

Goals for This Lesson
  • Design an original location that communicates its purpose, history, and feel through visual details
  • Draw establishing shots of 3 different environments: interior, exterior, and a wide landscape
  • Understand how architecture, props, and visual detail tell stories without words
  • Build a 'visual bible' page for a fictional world: key locations and design rules
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Location Design
  • Design a hero location for your story: sketch it from 3 angles
  • Fill a panel with a detailed interior — every prop should reveal something about who lives there
  • Draw an exterior establishing shot at 3 times of day: morning, noon, night
  • Create a visual contrast: a rich neighborhood vs. a poor neighborhood in the same world
Week 2: Visual Bible
  • Write and sketch a 1-page visual bible for your world: rules for architecture, fashion, tech level
  • Draw 3 locations from your world as 3-panel environment studies
  • Drop your characters into these environments — how do they interact with the space?
  • Add one location that reveals world lore without a single caption box
Key Concepts

Environments as Characters

The settings of your story should be as specific and designed as your characters

Generic environments are a missed storytelling opportunity. Every room, street, and landscape should tell us something about the people who inhabit it.

Ask of every location: what is its history? Who has power here? What is it used for? What is it hiding? The answers should be visible in the visual design.

A classic technique: design a location so that even without characters, a reader can guess what genre they're in (noir, sci-fi, fantasy) and what kind of story is being told.

Visual Research for World Design

Grounding fictional designs in observed reality

Study architecture photography for the settings you want to evoke. Even fantasy worlds benefit from observed detail — it creates believability.

Collect reference images organized by type: buildings, vehicles, furniture, clothing, technology. Keep these in a reference folder for your project.

The strongest fictional worlds blend the familiar and the strange. Ground your design in observed reality, then add the element that makes it your world.

The Establishing Shot

The wide shot that tells readers where they are before zooming in

A good establishing shot answers: where are we? What time is it? What's the mood? What are the power relationships of this space?

Don't rush past your establishing shots — a well-designed establishing shot does enormous world-building work and pays off for dozens of subsequent panels.

Revisit establishing shots when entering a location after a time skip — small changes to the environment can show the passage of time or change in character fortunes.

Props as Storytelling

Objects in scenes carry story information

A character's possessions reveal their personality, economic status, and history. A cluttered desk vs. a bare desk tells us entirely different stories.

Recurring props build symbolic weight: a specific photograph, a weapon, a piece of clothing. These objects can carry an entire arc of meaning.

Design props at the same level of specificity as your characters. Vague, generic objects feel like filler; specific, designed objects feel like a real world.

Writing Your Comic Script

Goals for This Lesson
  • Write a complete 8-page script in industry-standard format (full-script method)
  • Balance visual description with dialogue — know what the art will show vs. what text adds
  • Understand the difference between Marvel method, full script, and hybrid approaches
  • Script and then draw the same 4 pages — discover where your script and your art diverge
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Script Craft
  • Study 2 published scripts: Alan Moore (Watchmen script) and Brian Michael Bendis
  • Write a 2-page script in full-script format with detailed panel descriptions
  • Rewrite the same script Marvel-method (thumbnails + loose dialogue)
  • Identify: which method feels more natural for how you work?
Week 2: Script-to-Page
  • Write and complete a full 4-page script
  • Draw all 4 pages from your script exactly as written
  • Note every moment your art diverged from the script and why
  • Revise the script to match what the art actually needed
Key Concepts

Full-Script Format

The writer delivers complete, detailed instructions for every panel

Full script format: PAGE NUMBER, PANEL NUMBER, panel description, then each piece of dialogue/caption labeled by character.

Writers like Alan Moore and Grant Morrison use dense full scripts. This method is common when writer and artist are separate people who may not know each other well.

The risk of full script: over-directing the artist. Leave room for the artist to solve visual problems their own way — don't describe camera angles if the artist will figure those out better themselves.

Marvel Method

Artist draws from a plot synopsis; writer adds dialogue to finished art

Stan Lee and Jack Kirby developed this: Lee gives Kirby a plot outline, Kirby draws the full story, Lee adds dialogue and captions to the finished art.

Advantages: art drives the story, artists have freedom, the visual story is always coherent. Disadvantage: requires a writer who can write dialogue from finished art.

Many writer-artists use a hybrid: rough thumbnails with notes to themselves, then full dialogue added during or after the pencil stage.

Scene Structure vs. Beat Structure

Breaking story into scenes, then scenes into beats, then beats into panels

A scene has a beginning, middle, and end. A beat is a moment within a scene: a line of dialogue, a reaction, a discovery. A panel captures one beat.

Efficient comics scripting: map your 22-page issue as 6–8 scenes. Map each scene as 5–8 beats. Then assign panels to beats. This prevents pacing problems.

The 'page-turn reveal' rule: put your most dramatic beat — the twist, the revelation, the cliffhanger — at the bottom-right of a right-hand page. Readers see it only when they turn the page.

The Script as a Starting Point

Comics scripts are proposals, not blueprints — the artist completes them

Even with a full script, artists make thousands of decisions the script never addresses: exact pose, specific expression, background detail.

Great comics writer-artist partnerships work because both understand each other's strengths. Writer-artists resolve this by being both sides of the conversation.

When writing for another artist: write what the reader should feel, not what every panel should look like. 'Show us his grief' is better than 'the character's face is angled 30 degrees down-left with tears on cheek.'

Pacing & Rhythm

Goals for This Lesson
  • Control reading speed through panel count, panel size, and whitespace
  • Design pages that alternate between slow emotional beats and fast action
  • Understand how the page turn works as a narrative device
  • Re-pace an existing sequence: speed it up and slow it down through layout changes
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Pacing Study
  • Time yourself reading 5 pages from different genres — notice where you slow down and speed up
  • Take a 6-panel action sequence and re-draw it as 12 panels
  • Take a 6-panel quiet dialogue scene and re-draw it as 3 panels
  • Study Warren Ellis's use of 9-panel grids vs. David Aja's use in Hawkeye
Week 2: Rhythm Control
  • Design a 2-page spread: page 1 is fast-paced (6–8 small panels), page 2 is slow (1–2 large panels)
  • Design a page where the panel count accelerates through the page: 1, 2, 4, 4
  • Engineer a page-turn reveal: what's on the bottom-right of page A, and what does the reader find when they turn to page B?
  • Submit a complete 4-page sequence with deliberate pacing choices noted in the margins
Key Concepts

Panel Count and Pacing

More panels = faster read; fewer panels = slower read

A page with 8 small panels reads in seconds. A page with a single image demands the reader's time and attention.

This is your primary pacing tool. Before worrying about content, decide: does this scene need time to breathe (few, large panels) or does it need to feel frantic (many small panels)?

The 9-panel grid (used by Alan Moore in Watchmen) enforces rigid equality between panels — every beat gets the same time, creating a deliberate, metronomic feeling.

The Page Turn as a Storytelling Device

Every right-hand page ends with a cliffhanger that makes readers turn the page

The bottom-right panel of a right-hand page is the last thing a reader sees before turning. It should demand the turn: a question, a threat, a partial revelation.

The page-turn reveal: show something on page right bottom that makes sense one way, then the reader turns to find that meaning overturned.

Many novice comics are structured without considering page turns. Professionals plan entire issues around page-turn moments — they're the spine of the reading experience.

Splash Pages and Emotional Time

A full-page image creates a mandatory pause in reading

When you give a moment a full page, you force the reader to dwell. Use this for moments that deserve reflection: a death, a first meeting, a world-changing revelation.

The beat before and after a splash page matter. The panel leading in should create anticipation; the panel coming out should show consequence.

Over-using splash pages trains readers to flip past them. Save them for your 3–4 most important moments in a 22-page issue.

Eliminating Dead Time

Cut every panel that doesn't advance story, character, or atmosphere

'Dead time' is the interval between meaningful story beats — walking across a room, waiting, transitions. In film this is cut. In comics it's also cut, but more explicitly.

McCloud's 'moment-to-moment' transition is often dead time. Scene-to-scene and aspect-to-aspect transitions are often more dynamic choices.

The exception: 'dead time' panels are sometimes used intentionally to create unease or boredom that mirrors a character's experience. Use them consciously if you use them.

Genre & Visual Style

Goals for This Lesson
  • Identify the visual conventions of 4 major comics genres: superhero, noir, manga-influenced, and literary/indie
  • Articulate and sketch your own visual style — what choices will define your comics
  • Draw the same scene in 3 different genre styles
  • Write a 1-page 'visual style statement' for a project you want to make
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Genre Study
  • Read 30 pages each from a superhero comic, a noir comic, and an indie graphic novel
  • Draw 3 panels in superhero style: bold ink, dynamic angles, primary colors
  • Draw the same 3 panels in noir style: heavy blacks, moody lighting, limited palette
  • Draw the same 3 panels in a manga-influenced style: speed lines, expressive eyes, screen tone
Week 2: Style Development
  • Identify 5 artists whose work you're drawn to — list 3 specific things about each
  • Draw a scene combining techniques from 2 of those artists
  • Write a 1-page visual style statement: what visual choices will define your work?
  • Draw a 4-panel scene that feels unambiguously like YOU
Key Concepts

Genre Visual Conventions

Each genre has accumulated visual conventions readers expect

Superhero: high contrast inks, foreshortening, figure dynamism, saturated primary colors, angular letterforms, cityscape backgrounds.

Noir: heavy blacks, venetian blind shadows, rain-slicked streets, cool/desaturated palette, moody close-ups, femme fatale staging.

Indie/literary: loose linework, quieter color palettes, mundane settings elevated by careful observation, unconventional panel shapes, personal lettering.

Understanding Your Influences

How to learn from artists you admire without copying them

Identifying your influences is the first step toward your own style. The artist you love isn't their final image — they're the synthesis of their influences plus their unique choices.

Don't just copy artists: analyze WHY you respond to their work. Is it the line quality? The storytelling? The color choices? Isolate what resonates and bring that specific quality to your own work.

A useful exercise: draw a page in the style of an artist you love, then draw the same page your own way. Comparing the two reveals what you kept and what you changed — that difference is where your style lives.

Developing a Visual Voice

Your style is not decoration — it's how your worldview comes through the work

Style emerges from consistent choices: how you simplify forms, what details you include or exclude, how you handle line quality, what you find interesting to draw.

You don't choose a style — you develop it through thousands of drawings. But you can accelerate it by becoming conscious of your instinctive choices and leaning into them.

The goal isn't to develop a style that's unique for its own sake, but to develop a style that's genuinely yours — that can only tell the stories you want to tell, in the way only you can tell them.

Cross-Genre and Hybrid Styles

The most interesting work often blends genre conventions

Jeff Lemire's Essex County uses indie sensibility with superhero emotional scale. Saga blends sci-fi, fantasy, and literary slice-of-life.

Hybridizing genres creates freshness and signals to readers that you're not just executing a formula — you have something specific to say.

Your personal hybrid will emerge naturally from your interests. The key is to make the combination intentional and internally consistent, not accidental.

Portfolio & Publishing

Goals for This Lesson
  • Complete a polished 8–10 page short story ready to show or submit
  • Understand what publishers, editors, and collaborators look for in a comics portfolio
  • Know the major publishing pathways and which is right for your goals
  • Build a simple online presence: a place to share your comics with the world
Two-Week Plan
Week 1: Polish Your Story
  • Finish pencils, inks, color, and lettering on your 8–10 page story
  • Have 3 people read it and give feedback — revise based on the clearest patterns
  • Scan or photograph all pages at high resolution (600dpi or higher)
  • Create a PDF version formatted at comic-standard dimensions
Week 2: Portfolio and Presence
  • Build an online portfolio: Tumblr, Instagram, Webtoon, or a personal site
  • Post your short story publicly with a clear title and short description
  • Research 3 anthologies, open submissions, or contests to submit to
  • Write your logline: one sentence that captures your story and makes someone want to read it
Key Concepts

What Editors and Publishers Want

Understanding your audience when submitting your work

Editors at publishers want to see: clear storytelling, consistent character drawing, professional lettering, and evidence you can sustain a story over multiple pages.

A polished 5–8 page sample story is the standard submission material. It should be your best work, showing a complete narrative arc.

Common rejection reason: the art is strong but the storytelling is unclear. Editors care more about clear story than impressive individual panels.

The Publishing Pathways

Different routes to getting your comics in front of readers

Self-publishing on the web (Webtoon, Tapas, social media) is free and immediate — start now, build an audience, and learn from reader feedback.

Kickstarter and crowdfunding: viable for a finished physical comic. Requires an existing audience. Average successful campaign has 200–500 backers.

Publisher submission: research each publisher's open submission windows and requirements. Small press publishers (Fantagraphics, Drawn & Quarterly, Top Shelf) publish literary and indie work; Big 2 have talent search programs.

Anthology Submissions

Short story anthologies are the best entry point for new comics artists

Anthologies accept complete short stories (typically 6–12 pages) and are published on themes or genres. They're low-risk, high-learning for new creators.

Publishing in an anthology builds your resume, gets your work seen by editors, and connects you with other creators. Many successful comics careers started with anthology appearances.

Find anthologies via Comics Beat, Bleeding Cool, or social media. Many are run by cartoonists rather than publishers — the community is accessible.

Building an Online Presence

Your work needs a home on the internet

The easiest start: Instagram or Tumblr for individual pages; Webtoon or Tapas for full serialized comics. Use at least one consistently.

Posting process content (sketches, WIPs, model sheets) builds an audience even before your story is complete. People invest in creators they know.

A personal website with a custom domain (e.g. youname.com) looks professional for publisher and convention submissions. Squarespace or Cargo are popular options for comics artists.
